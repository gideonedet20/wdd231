Before you go

Getting ready for a park visit

National parks and urban parks work differently — permits and guides for one, just a gate fee for the other. Here's what changes between them.

Eight essentials

What to sort out before you set out

1Entry permits

Federal national parks are managed by the National Park Service and charge a gate fee plus, for some, a camera or vehicle permit. Urban parks like Lekki Conservation Centre or Millennium Park charge a simple entry fee.

2Best time to go

The dry season, roughly November to March, gives the clearest roads and the easiest wildlife viewing in the savanna parks. Rainforest reserves like Cross River and Okomu stay green and walkable most of the year, but trails get slippery in the wet season.

3Guides & rangers

For national parks and reserves, a registered park guide isn't optional in practice — trails are often unmarked and wildlife encounters are safer with someone who knows the terrain. Urban parks don't require one.

4Getting there

Most national parks sit well outside city centres, so budget for a full day of travel each way if driving from a major hub. Urban parks are the easier half-day option when time is tight.

5Where to stay

Parks like Yankari, Obudu, and Kainji Lake have on-site lodges or chalets that fill up around holidays — book ahead. For a day trip to an urban park, no lodging is needed at all.

6What to pack

Neutral-coloured clothing and closed shoes for game drives and forest trails; a light jacket for highland spots like Obudu; insect repellent and sun protection everywhere; and a power bank for the more remote parks.

7Wildlife etiquette

Keep a respectful distance from animals, stay inside vehicles where advised, and follow your guide's lead on noise and movement — especially in gorilla and chimpanzee habitat like Cross River and Gashaka-Gumti.

8Connectivity

Phone signal drops out inside most of the larger parks. Download offline maps and let someone know your route before heading into a remote reserve.

Common questions

Quick answers before you book

Do I need a permit for every park?

Federal national parks generally require a gate permit purchased on-site or through the park office. Urban and state-run parks usually just charge a flat entry fee at the gate.

Can I visit more than one state's park in a trip?

Yes, especially within a zone — Cross River state alone pairs a rainforest reserve with a highland resort. Crossing between distant zones takes longer; plan around a domestic flight for those legs.

Are the urban parks free?

Most charge a modest entry fee rather than being fully free, partly to fund upkeep. It's still far less than a national park gate fee and permit combined.

Is it safe to self-drive to a park?

Road conditions and current safety guidance vary by state — check an up-to-date travel advisory for your specific route before self-driving to a remote park.

A note on this guide

Permit rules, fees, and safety conditions change and vary by park and by current circumstances. Treat the points above as a planning checklist, not a substitute for checking with the relevant park office or a current travel advisory shortly before you go.
